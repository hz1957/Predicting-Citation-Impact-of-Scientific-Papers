1. Introduction & Background

The number of citations a paper accrues is a widely used—if imperfect—proxy for scientific influ- ence [1]. Citation dynamics are extremely skewed: in computer science, the most-cited 7% of papers collect over half of all citations while roughly 21% go uncited (Gini ≈ 0.73, Fig. 1) [7]—a concen- tration that has only intensified as AI publication volume has surged [2, 6]. With thousands of AI papers posted each month, this imbalance makes it hard to tell early on which work will matter; a model that forecasts impact at (or shortly after) publication could help researchers, reviewers, and libraries surface promising papers before citations accumulate, and reveal which characteristics drive attention. Text alone is a relatively weak predictor of impact—the strongest text-based models leave most of the variance unexplained [8]—while bibliometric and metadata signals available near publication consistently add substantial predictive power [1]. In this project, We study citation prediction for AI/ML papers—a fast-moving field with a large, openly catalogued literature—and use feature-importance analysis to learn which signals actually drive predicted impact, rather than assuming them in advance.

For this midterm milestone, we focus on establishing a complete, leakage-free machine learning pipeline. After preprocessing the dataset and constructing text and metadata representations, we implement one representative unsupervised learning algorithm (K-Means clustering) and two baseline supervised learning models(Ridge Regression and Logistic Regression). These baseline models provide an initial evaluation of the predictive capability of the engineered features and establish a foundation for comparison with more advanced machine learning methods in the final project.

Dataset

We assemble the dataset from open sources requiring no API keys: a HuggingFace arXiv corpus (e.g. CShorten/ML-ArXiv-Papers / arxiv-community/arxiv_dataset) provides ti- tles, abstracts, authors, and dates with a one-line load_dataset call, which we enrich with citation counts and affiliations from OpenAlex [3] (key-free REST API, batched by arXiv ID/DOI). We restrict to AI/ML categories (cs.LG, cs.CL, cs.CV) and sample ∼50,000 papers from 2018–2022 so citations have had time to mature. We also investigate whether the learned predictive signals generalize beyond the AI/ML literature. Before model development, the collected data are prepro- cessed and transformed into text and metadata feature representations to improve computational efficiency and ensure a leakage-free learning pipeline. Details of the preprocessing procedure are provided in the Methods section.

Data sources:

  • HuggingFace ML-ArXiv Papers Dataset
  • OpenAlex Scholarly Database

2. Problem Definition

Problem. Given features describing a scientific paper at publication time (title, abstract, venue, author count, reference count, field, and year), predict its fixed-window citation count. This is formulated as a regression problem.

We also investigate a binary classification formulation: whether a paper becomes highly cited, defined as belonging to the top decile of fixed-window citations within its field and publication year.

Motivation. Reliable early estimates of scientific impact could help researchers, libraries, and reviewers prioritize rapidly growing literature. Furthermore, feature importance analysis reveals which characteristics influence eventual citation outcomes.

Citation prediction also provides an opportunity to study biases embedded in citation systems. Therefore, predictions are interpreted descriptively rather than as measures of scientific merit.

3. Methods

Dataset and Preprocessing

The dataset consists of approximately 150,000 AI and machine learning research papers collected from the arXiv categories cs.LG, cs.CL, and cs.CV. Each record contains bibliographic metadata, publication venue, author information, citation information, and extracted textual features.

The papers are divided into three temporal cohorts according to publication year. The primary experiments use the C1 cohort, where earlier papers are used for training and later papers are reserved for testing. This temporal split prevents information leakage and provides a realistic prediction setting.

Cohort Years Papers Target Purpose
C1 2018--2022 49,999 Citations within first 3 years Training / Evaluation
C2 2023--2024 50,000 Citations within first 1 year Generalization
C3 2025--2026 49,974 Citations to date Forecasting

Citation counts exhibit a strongly skewed distribution. Therefore, the prediction target is transformed using:

\[ y=\log(1+\text{target\_citations}) \]

Only information available at publication time is used for feature construction. Citation-derived variables that could introduce target leakage are excluded.

Paper titles and abstracts are converted into TF-IDF vectors and compressed into a 100-dimensional latent semantic representation using Truncated Singular Value Decomposition (LSA).

Metadata features including publication year, author count, reference count, venue frequency, document length, affiliation statistics, and arXiv category indicators are standardized using StandardScaler. All preprocessing steps are fitted only on the training split and then applied unchanged to validation and testing data.

Unsupervised Learning: K-Means Clustering

Unsupervised learning is performed on the C1 training split to discover latent topic structures and citation-impact archetypes. Two K-Means analyses are conducted: one using the combined text and metadata representation and another using only the LSA text representation. The optimal number of clusters is determined using the Silhouette coefficient and Davies--Bouldin index. Principal Component Analysis (PCA), UMAP, and t-SNE are employed to visualize the learned clusters and examine their relationship with citation impact.

Following the proposal, we ran K-Means on the assembled feature vectors of the c1 train split (24,691 papers, 2018–2020; the temporal test split is held out). Text features are TF-IDF over title + abstract (13,775 terms) compressed to 100 dimensions with Truncated SVD and L2-normalized (LSA); the metadata block (author/reference counts, text lengths, year, frequency-encoded venue, category indicators) is standardized. We cluster twice with the same LSA text features: once with the metadata block concatenated (the proposal's assembled vectors) and once with the text features alone. Citation outcomes are used only to profile clusters, never as clustering features.

Reason for K-Means Clustering

The proposed methods were selected to provide interpretable baseline models while remaining computationally efficient for a dataset containing approximately 150,000 research papers. K-Means was chosen as the primary unsupervised learning algorithm because it scales well to high-dimensional datasets, has relatively low computational complexity, and produces easily interpretable clusters through centroid representations. Since the text features are represented in a latent semantic space after TF--IDF and Truncated Singular Value Decomposition (LSA), Euclidean distance becomes a reasonable similarity measure, making K-Means an appropriate clustering method. Cluster quality is assessed using the Silhouette coefficient and Davies--Bouldin index, which provide complementary measures of cluster cohesion and separation.

PCA, UMAP, and t-SNE were selected for visualization because each reveals different characteristics of the learned feature space. PCA provides a linear projection that preserves global variance and serves as a baseline visualization. UMAP preserves both local and global neighborhood structures while scaling efficiently to large datasets, whereas t-SNE is effective at revealing local cluster structure despite its higher computational cost. Using all three methods provides a more comprehensive qualitative assessment of the discovered topic archetypes.

Cluster quality is evaluated using complementary metrics:

  • Silhouette coefficient: measures cluster cohesion and separation.
  • Davies--Bouldin index: evaluates similarity between clusters.

PCA, UMAP, and t-SNE provide complementary visualization perspectives. PCA preserves global variance, UMAP captures local and global neighborhood structures efficiently, and t-SNE highlights local cluster organization.

Supervised Learning: Ridge and Logistic Regression

Two linear baseline models are implemented:

  • Ridge Regression: predicts log-transformed citation counts.
  • Logistic Regression: identifies highly cited papers using a binary classification threshold.

Both models use the same leakage-free feature representation described above and are trained exclusively on the C1 training cohort.

The Ridge Regression model uses:

  • Regularization parameter: \(\alpha=1.0\)

The Logistic Regression classifier uses:

  • C=1.0
  • class_weight="balanced"
  • max_iter=2000

Performance is evaluated using regression, ranking, and classification metrics. In addition, cluster assignments from K-Means are incorporated as engineered features through an ablation study to determine whether unsupervised topic information improves supervised prediction.

Reason for Regression Method

Ridge Regression and Logistic Regression provide interpretable baseline models for high-dimensional sparse features.

Ridge Regression reduces overfitting through L2 regularization while maintaining continuous citation prediction capability. Logistic Regression provides probabilistic predictions for identifying highly cited papers and serves as a transparent classification baseline.

These baseline models establish a reference point for evaluating future nonlinear approaches such as tree-based ensembles and graph neural networks.

4. Results & Discussion

K-Means Clustering

The unsupervised learning pipeline was designed to investigate whether latent semantic represen- tations of research papers naturally form meaningful groups and whether these groups exhibit different citation characteristics. Given summarizes the overall workflow. After constructing the 100-dimensional LSA representation from TF–IDF features, two different K-Means analyses were performed: one using only the text representation and another using the combined text and standardized metadata features. Cluster quality was evaluated over multiple values of k using the Silhouette coefficient, while PCA, UMAP, and t-SNE were used to visualize the learned feature space

Such compares the Silhouette scores obtained with and without metadata. When metadata features were incorporated, the score exhibited a clear interior maximum at k = 3, indicating the presence of three well-separated coarse groups. In contrast, the text-only representation showed the gradual increase in Silhouette score that is commonly observed in high-dimensional text clustering. Rather than selecting the largest score, k = 8 was chosen because it provided the finest partition in which every cluster remained interpretable and sufficiently populated. Consequently, two com- plementary clustering analyses were retained: a coarse three-cluster partition for identifying broad research domains and a finer eight-cluster partition for discovering topical impact archetypes.

More details of decinsion makings are followed; With text + metadata features the silhouette score has a clear interior maximum at k = 3: the three clusters match the arXiv category split almost exactly (99.9% cs.CV, 100% cs.CL, and the remaining cs.LG mass), and their outcomes already differ — 14.7% of cs.CV papers reach the corpus-wide top decile of 3-year citations versus 7.6% of the cs.LG-and-other cluster. Using the same text (LSA) features without the metadata block, the silhouette instead rises slowly and monotonically with k — typical for text — so we fix k = 8, the largest k at which every cluster remains interpretable and well populated. This second clustering is run independently on the full corpus (not nested inside the k = 3 clusters); its clusters mostly split the three category groups further but can also cut across them (e.g. the GAN / adversarial cluster spans cs.CV and cs.LG)

To better understand the geometry of the learned feature space, PCA, UMAP, and t-SNE pro- jections were generated using the fitted embeddings. Mapping illustrates the continuous citation distribution in the reduced-dimensional space, where each point is colored by the logarithm of its three-year citation count. Although citation information was never used during feature construction or clustering, regions containing highly cited papers are visibly concentrated rather than uniformly distributed throughout the embedding space. This observation suggests that the semantic text representation alone already captures meaningful information related to future citation impact

Metadata-Based \(k=3\) Clustering

Given feature presents the visualization of the k = 3 clustering using PCA, UMAP, and t-SNE for the combined text and metadata representation. All three embedding methods reveal consistent cluster boundaries despite preserving different geometric properties. PCA preserves the global variance of the feature space, whereas UMAP and t-SNE emphasize local neighborhood relationships. Across all three projections, the three clusters are relatively well separated, indicating that the addition of metadata introduces strong structural information. Examination of the cluster profiles shows that these groups closely correspond to the primary arXiv categories (cs.CV, cs.CL, and cs.LG/Others), demonstrating that K-Means successfully recovers the major research domains without using cate- gory labels during training.

Text-Only \(k=8\) Topic Archetypes

For \(k=8\) was selected because it provides a finer partition while maintaining interpretable and sufficiently populated clusters. It shows the corresponding visualization for the text-only representation with k = 8. Compared with the coarse partition, the clusters are more diffuse and partially overlap, reflecting the continuous nature of research topics. Nevertheless, several distinct semantic regions emerge consis- tently across PCA, UMAP, and t-SNE. Inspection of the highest-weight TF–IDF terms identifies recognizable research themes, including object detection and 3D vision, reinforcement learning, optimization theory, applied machine learning, deep neural architectures, GANs and adversarial learning, image segmentation, and natural language processing. Since these topic labels are as- signed only after clustering by examining representative terms, the discovered structure is entirely data driven.

Clustered outcomes for eight topical clusters with sharply different outcomes The text-only clusters are cleanly topical: 3D & object detection (C0), reinforcement learning (C1), applied ML & prediction (C2), optimization & theory (C3), deep architectures (C4), GANs & adversarial robustness (C5), image segmentation (C6), and NLP & speech (C7) — names assigned by hand from each cluster's top TF-IDF terms. UMAP and t-SNE agree on the same topical islands, an encouraging robustness check, while linear PCA shows the same coarse topic gradient with heavy overlap — the nonlinear methods are what make the structure visible. Citation outcomes separate strongly: 3D & object detection does best (median 7 citations in 3 years; 16.0% reach the corpus top decile), followed by segmentation (11.8%) and NLP (11.3%); optimization-theory papers sit lowest (median 3; 4.0%; 16% uncited). NLP is the heavy-tail cluster — mean 47.6 versus median 6 citations, reflecting a few transformer-era mega-hits — which reinforces the log-transformed target. Metadata profiles add texture: theory papers have markedly fewer authors (z = −0.40), vision papers more.

The visualization indicates that highly cited papers are not uniformly distributed throughout the feature space. Instead, they appear concentrated within specific semantic regions, suggesting that textual representation captures meaningful information about future scientific impact.

An important observation is that these text-defined archetypes exhibit markedly different citation behavior. Vision-related topics, segmentation, GANs, and NLP contain substantially larger fractions of highly cited papers, whereas optimization-oriented papers receive comparatively fewer citations. The NLP cluster displays a particularly heavy-tailed citation distribution, with a small number of transformer-era papers accounting for exceptionally large citation counts. These findings indicate that topic membership alone contains predictive information regarding future scientific impact, even though citation information was never used during clustering.

Overall, the unsupervised analysis demonstrates that the proposed feature representation captures both coarse disciplinary structure and fine-grained topical organization. The resulting K-Means clusters provide interpretable impact archetypes and motivate their subsequent use as engineered features for the supervised learning models.

Summary of K-Means Findings

  • The coarse clustering using text and metadata features (\(k=3\)) closely matches major arXiv research categories. Citation outcomes differ between groups, with Computer Vision showing approximately 15% top-decile papers, Computational Language approximately 11%, and Machine Learning/Other approximately 8%.
  • The finer text-only clustering (\(k=8\)) identifies interpretable topic archetypes. Vision, segmentation, NLP, and GAN-related clusters exhibit higher citation impact, while optimization theory forms the lowest-impact cluster.
  • The NLP cluster exhibits a heavy-tailed citation distribution, with a mean of 47.6 citations but a median of only 6 citations, demonstrating why log-transformed targets are necessary.
  • The relationship between semantic topic structure and citation impact suggests that text representations provide predictive information before citation variables are introduced.

5. Conclusion and Future Work

This project develops a machine learning framework for predicting scientific citation impact using publicly available paper metadata and textual representations.

The initial unsupervised analysis demonstrates that semantic representations capture meaningful research structures and reveal topic-dependent citation patterns.

Future work will extend the supervised models using nonlinear approaches, including ensemble learning methods, hyperparameter optimization, and graph-based models incorporating citation and collaboration networks.

Ethics & Sustainability

Citation counts contain known biases related to venue prestige, field size, language effects, and demographic disparities. Therefore, predicted impact should not be interpreted as a measure of scientific merit.

The model is intended as a descriptive analytical tool for understanding citation dynamics rather than a decision-making system for evaluating researchers or allocating resources.

Because this analysis is observational, feature importance results represent associations rather than causal relationships. Additionally, citation labels are dependent on the selected data source and may contain incomplete coverage.
