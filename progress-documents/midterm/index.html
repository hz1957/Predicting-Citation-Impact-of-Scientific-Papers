1. Introduction & Background

The number of citations a paper receives is a widely used, although imperfect, indicator of scientific influence. Citation distributions are highly skewed: a small fraction of papers accumulate a disproportionate fraction of total citations, while many papers receive limited attention. In computer science, the most-cited papers account for a large portion of total citations, and a substantial fraction of publications remain uncited. This concentration makes early prediction of scientific impact challenging but valuable.

With the rapid growth of artificial intelligence and machine learning research, thousands of new papers are published every month. A model capable of estimating future citation impact shortly after publication could help researchers, reviewers, and digital libraries identify emerging research directions and understand which characteristics are associated with increased scientific attention.

Previous studies indicate that textual information alone provides limited predictive capability, while bibliometric and metadata information available at publication time can significantly improve prediction accuracy. Therefore, this project investigates citation prediction for AI/ML research papers by combining textual representations with publication metadata and analyzing which features contribute to future citation impact.

For this midterm milestone, we establish a complete leakage-free machine learning pipeline. The dataset is processed into text and metadata feature representations, followed by unsupervised learning using K-Means clustering and baseline supervised models including Ridge Regression and Logistic Regression. The unsupervised analysis identifies latent research structures and citation-impact archetypes, while the supervised models provide an initial assessment of citation prediction capability.

Dataset

The dataset is constructed from publicly available scholarly sources. A HuggingFace arXiv corpus provides paper titles, abstracts, authors, and publication information. Citation information and additional bibliometric metadata are obtained from OpenAlex. The dataset is restricted to AI/ML research categories: cs.LG, cs.CL, and cs.CV.

Approximately 150,000 papers published between 2018 and 2026 are collected. The data are separated into temporal cohorts to prevent information leakage and evaluate realistic prediction performance.

2. Problem Definition

Problem. Given information available at publication time, including title, abstract, publication metadata, author information, references, and research category, predict the future citation impact of a scientific paper.

The primary formulation is regression, where the objective is predicting a fixed-window citation count. Because citation distributions are highly skewed, the target variable is transformed as:

\[ y=\log(1+\mathrm{citations}) \]

A secondary classification formulation is also investigated, where papers are classified according to whether they belong to the top citation decile within their publication year and research field.

3. Methods

Dataset and Preprocessing

Only information available at publication time is used during feature construction. Citation-derived variables that could introduce target leakage are excluded.

Paper titles and abstracts are transformed using TF-IDF. The resulting sparse text representation is compressed into a 100-dimensional latent semantic space using Truncated Singular Value Decomposition (LSA).

Metadata features include publication year, author count, reference count, document length, venue frequency, affiliation statistics, and arXiv category indicators. These variables are standardized using StandardScaler. All preprocessing operations are fitted only on the training data and then applied unchanged to validation and testing sets.

Unsupervised Learning: K-Means Clustering

The unsupervised analysis is performed on the C1 training split to discover latent research structures and citation-impact archetypes. Two independent K-Means clustering experiments are conducted.

  • Text + Metadata clustering: The 100-dimensional LSA representation is combined with standardized metadata features to identify broad research domains.
  • Text-only clustering: Only the LSA representation is used to discover finer-grained semantic topic archetypes.

Citation outcomes are never included as clustering features. Instead, citation statistics are used only after clustering to characterize the discovered groups.

The optimal number of clusters is selected using the Silhouette coefficient and Davies--Bouldin index. PCA, UMAP, and t-SNE projections are used to visualize cluster structure and compare citation distributions across the embedding space.

Reason for K-Means Clustering

K-Means is selected because it provides an efficient and interpretable baseline for large-scale high-dimensional datasets. After TF-IDF compression using LSA, papers are represented in a continuous semantic space where distance-based clustering is appropriate.

Cluster centroids provide interpretable representations of dominant research themes, allowing cluster assignments and distances to centroids to be used as potential engineered features for downstream supervised models.

Supervised Learning: Ridge and Logistic Regression

Two linear baseline models are implemented:

  • Ridge Regression: predicts the logarithmically transformed citation count.
  • Logistic Regression: predicts whether a paper becomes highly cited.

These models provide interpretable baselines for evaluating whether engineered text and metadata features contain predictive information before applying more complex nonlinear approaches.

4. Results & Discussion

K-Means Clustering

The unsupervised learning analysis investigates whether the learned semantic representation of research papers naturally forms meaningful groups and whether these groups exhibit different citation behaviors. After converting titles and abstracts into TF-IDF representations and reducing them to a 100-dimensional LSA space, two independent K-Means clustering analyses were performed:

  • A combined representation using LSA text features and standardized metadata.
  • A text-only representation using only the LSA semantic features.

Citation information was excluded during clustering and was only used afterward to profile the resulting groups. This prevents target leakage while allowing the relationship between research topics and citation outcomes to be examined.

The model selection results show substantially different clustering behavior depending on the feature representation. When metadata features are included, the Silhouette coefficient reaches a clear interior maximum at \(k=3\). These three clusters correspond closely to broad research domains represented by the major arXiv categories: cs.CV, cs.CL, and cs.LG.

In contrast, clustering using only text features produces a smoother increase in Silhouette score as the number of clusters increases. Since high-dimensional text spaces naturally contain continuous semantic variation, the maximum Silhouette value alone does not necessarily represent the most meaningful partition. Therefore, \(k=8\) was selected as the largest value where all clusters remained sufficiently populated and interpretable.

Metadata-Based \(k=3\) Research Domain Clustering

The combined text and metadata representation produces three clearly separated groups in PCA, UMAP, and t-SNE projections. Although the clustering algorithm does not receive category labels, the discovered groups closely reproduce the major AI research divisions.

The cluster profiles reveal different citation behaviors among these research domains. Computer vision-oriented papers contain a larger fraction of highly cited papers, followed by computational language and machine learning groups. This indicates that metadata information introduces strong structural signals that correspond not only to research organization but also to differences in citation patterns.

This result demonstrates that publication metadata contains meaningful information beyond textual similarity. However, because the clusters primarily represent broad disciplinary categories, a finer text-only clustering analysis is required to identify more specific citation-impact archetypes.

Text-Only \(k=8\) Topic Archetypes

The text-only clustering produces finer semantic structures that correspond to recognizable research topics. Examination of representative TF-IDF terms allows the clusters to be interpreted as:

  • C0: 3D vision and object detection
  • C1: Reinforcement learning
  • C2: Applied machine learning and prediction
  • C3: Optimization and theoretical methods
  • C4: Deep neural architectures
  • C5: GANs and adversarial learning
  • C6: Image segmentation
  • C7: Natural language processing and speech

The clusters are not perfectly separated because research topics exist on a continuous spectrum. However, consistent structures appear across PCA, UMAP, and t-SNE visualizations, indicating that the semantic representation captures meaningful topical organization.

When the embedding space is colored according to citation impact, highly cited papers are concentrated within specific semantic regions. Although citation labels were never used during feature construction or clustering, the presence of high-impact regions suggests that text representations contain predictive information regarding future citation outcomes.

The discovered topic archetypes exhibit substantially different citation profiles. Vision-related clusters, segmentation, GANs, and NLP contain larger fractions of highly cited papers, whereas optimization and theory-oriented papers show lower citation impact. The NLP cluster exhibits particularly strong citation skew, with a high average citation count caused by a small number of transformer-era papers receiving extremely large attention.

This heavy-tailed behavior reinforces the motivation for logarithmic citation targets in supervised learning. It also demonstrates that topic membership alone provides useful information about future scientific impact, even before introducing citation-derived variables.

Implications for Supervised Learning

The unsupervised analysis provides several insights for the supervised prediction stage. First, the LSA text representation contains substantial signal related to citation outcome. Second, cluster assignments and distances to cluster centroids can be incorporated as additional engineered features. Finally, evaluating prediction errors by cluster may reveal whether models perform differently across research areas with different citation dynamics.

Overall, K-Means successfully identifies both broad disciplinary structures through metadata-enhanced clustering and finer topical impact archetypes through text-only clustering. These results motivate the integration of unsupervised representations into future supervised citation prediction models.

Regression

Supervised regression experiments evaluate whether engineered text, metadata, and clustering features can predict future citation impact. Ridge Regression serves as an interpretable baseline, while additional nonlinear models will be evaluated during the final stage.

Regression performance, feature importance analysis, and comparison with advanced models will be incorporated after completion of the final evaluation stage.

5. Conclusion and Future Work

This project develops a machine learning framework for predicting scientific citation impact using publicly available scholarly metadata and textual representations. A leakage-free pipeline is established by combining temporal data splitting, LSA-based text representations, standardized metadata features, and citation-based prediction targets.

The unsupervised analysis demonstrates that research papers contain meaningful latent structures before citation information is introduced. Metadata-enhanced K-Means clustering identifies broad research domains, while text-only clustering reveals finer topical impact archetypes with distinct citation behaviors. These results indicate that semantic representations contain useful information for understanding future citation outcomes.

Future work will focus on improving supervised prediction performance through nonlinear machine learning approaches, including Random Forest, XGBoost, hyperparameter optimization, and graph-based models incorporating citation and collaboration networks. In addition, cluster assignments discovered during the unsupervised analysis will be evaluated as engineered features to determine whether topic-aware representations improve citation prediction accuracy.

Ethics & Sustainability

Citation counts represent one measurable aspect of scholarly attention but do not directly measure research quality, originality, or scientific value. Citation systems contain known biases related to research field size, venue visibility, language, collaboration networks, and publication practices.

Therefore, predicted citation impact should be interpreted as an analytical estimate of citation dynamics rather than a ranking mechanism for evaluating individual researchers, institutions, or funding decisions.

Because this study is observational, feature importance results identify statistical associations rather than causal relationships. Furthermore, citation labels depend on the selected bibliographic database and may contain incomplete coverage or missing information.
