1. Introduction & Dataset Status

The exponential growth of the scientific literature in Artificial Intelligence and Machine Learning (AI/ML) makes it increasingly difficult for researchers to parse and surface high-impact research programmatically. In this project, we predict the future citation impact of scientific papers at their time of publication using metadata features and textual indicators.

Since our proposal, we have fully assembled and cleaned our target dataset. We compiled a robust corpus of 150,000 papers from arXiv categories cs.LG (Machine Learning), cs.CL (Computation and Language), and cs.CV (Computer Vision) published between 2018 and 2022. By leveraging the open APIs of OpenAlex and Semantic Scholar, we enriched each paper with actual citation counts, author publication histories (including individual h-indices), and venue rankings. Restricting the timeline ensures that even the youngest papers in our cohort have a mature citation window of at least 3.5 years.

2. Preprocessing & Feature Engineering

Predicting citation counts is a challenging regression task primarily because citation distributions are notoriously heavy-tailed and heavily skewed. To address this, we executed a dedicated prep pipeline:

  • Target Transformation: We applied a log-transform, y = log(1 + citations), to normalize the output space, stabilize numerical gradients, and prevent a handful of highly-cited outlier papers from dominating our loss functions.
  • Textual Feature Engineering: Titles and abstracts were converted into high-dimensional semantic representations. We compared a TF-IDF baseline compressed via TruncatedSVD to dense 768-dimensional sentence-level SciBERT embeddings to capture domain-specific semantic concepts.
  • Metadata Extraction: Numeric features (author count, reference count, and text length metrics) were scaled using StandardScaler. Categorical variables (such as target journal/conference venues and primary categories) were frequency-encoded and scaled.
  • Leakage Prevention: To ensure realistic out-of-sample evaluations, we partition our data into three distinct temporal cohorts (train, validation, and test based on publication year). This completely prevents look-ahead bias and ensures that author h-index statistics are computed only using information available strictly prior to a paper's publication date.

3. Unsupervised Exploration

To discover underlying structures in our feature space, we performed K-Means clustering on the combined metadata and textual representations. Using the Silhouette Coefficient and Elbow method, we determined that k = 4 clusters provided a highly descriptive partitioning of our dataset:

  1. Cluster 0 (Methodological Core): Dense theoretical papers with high reference counts, primarily in cs.LG.
  2. Cluster 1 (Interdisciplinary Applications): Highly collaborative papers with large author counts bridging CV/CL to physical sciences.
  3. Cluster 2 (Niche/Preprint Ecosystem): Preprints with lower citation velocity and lower baseline author h-indices.
  4. Cluster 3 (High-Prestige Anchors): Papers published in flagship venues (e.g., CVPR, NeurIPS) characterized by high initial citation rates.

We visualized these high-dimensional structures in two dimensions using t-SNE and UMAP. The resulting mappings demonstrate clear spatial separation among our identified clusters, validating that our preprocessing and feature representations carry meaningful structure.

4. Supervised Learning Progress

We developed and evaluated three distinct regressors of varying model capacity to predict log(1 + citations):

  • Ridge Regression: Served as our linear baseline, offering high interpretability and quick execution.
  • Support Vector Regression (SVR): Configured with a radial basis function (RBF) kernel to map complex, non-linear relationships.
  • Random Forest Regressor: A powerful ensemble model consisting of 150 estimators to handle interactions among metadata and textual features.

Our models were evaluated on the validation cohort using standard regression metrics: Root Mean Squared Error (RMSE), Mean Absolute Error (MAE), and Coefficient of Determination ($R^2$).

5. Preliminary Results & Discussion

The performance of our baseline models is summarized below. The Random Forest Regressor demonstrated the strongest performance, achieving an $R^2$ of 0.605 on the validation set, significantly outperforming the linear Ridge model ($R^2 = 0.412$).

Model Architecture Validation MAE Validation RMSE Validation R²
Ridge Regression (Baseline) 0.684 0.895 0.412
Support Vector Regression (RBF Kernel) 0.592 0.791 0.539
Random Forest Regressor 0.511 0.723 0.605

Feature Importance & Insights: Our initial SHAP (SHapley Additive exPlanations) analysis indicates that venue impact factor/prestige and the lead author's existing h-index are the two most powerful predictors of early citation trajectory. Interestingly, text embeddings alone (from both TF-IDF and SciBERT) provide a reliable baseline but struggle to capture the high-end extremes of citation skew on their own, proving that contextual metadata is critical for accurate impact prediction.

Next Steps & Future Work

Moving forward into the final project phase, we will focus on:

  • Hyperparameter Optimization: Utilizing random and grid searches to tune max depth, estimators, and learning rates for XGBoost and LightGBM models.
  • Graph Neural Networks (GNNs): Exploring the inclusion of a co-authorship and reference citation network to run graph-based node regression, capturing relational features that tree models miss.
  • Ablation Studies: Systematically stripping away feature groups (e.g., text-only vs. metadata-only) to precisely isolate where the predictive power is generated.
